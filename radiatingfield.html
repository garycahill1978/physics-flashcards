When a charge moves, when does its field change?

A charge does not release its entire electric field like a detached object. Changes in its motion propagate outwards at the speed of light.

Try these demonstrations

1. Bump and return: A finite change propagates away even after the charge is back where it started.

2. Move and stay: Close to the charge, the field reflects its new position; far away it still reflects the old one until the update arrives.

3. Position, then oscillate: Click Position charge, drag the red source anywhere, and then choose Oscillate. This is a new starting condition; for a physical moving-charge disturbance, drag the source during another experiment.

4. Test-charge scattering: Enable the test charges and their secondary radiation. They are bound-charge models, not free particles.

5. Radiation fall-off: Select Radiation: near & far on the graph. Both electric-field traces use the same vertical scale; the red source-motion trace has different units.

How to read the picture: These are instantaneous electric-field integral curves, not physical strings attached to the charge. The deformation travels because the field at each point depends on the charge’s earlier motion. The field lines are traced to the view boundary rather than given an artificial fixed length. Dragging during an experiment preserves the past field and produces delayed changes; Position charge instead prepares a new stationary initial condition without claiming that a charge teleports physically. Physics and limitations: This is a dimensionless demonstration of the Liénard–Wiechert electric field of a prescribed, sub-light-speed point charge, including velocity (1/r²) and acceleration (1/r) terms. The field is evaluated at the appropriate retarded time. Optional dashed circles are light-travel-time guides, not physical detached electric-field rings. Positive and negative test charges represent spring-bound charges responding to changes in the source field, with a user-adjustable display-response gain. They can re-radiate weak acceleration-dependent fields, visualised as outward-moving wavefront segments and calculated using a retarded non-relativistic 1/r approximation. Secondary fields are shown with amplified opacity; they do not drive the primary source, and multiple scattering / radiation reaction are omitted. Field-line displacement is not a measure of electric-field magnitude: the radiative-field vectors and near/far graph show actual relative field strengths in the model. Source charge, test charges and field strengths are not drawn to scale; no quantum photons are included. Distances and seconds are illustrative model units. Animation times are greatly slowed down relative to light propagation in nature.
